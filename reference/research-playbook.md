# Research playbook

The goal is not to learn about the company. It's to find **the door** — the
specific group inside it that does the work, and one sentence proving you know
it's there.

## The four questions

Research each employer until you can answer all four. If you can't, say so in
the card rather than filling the gap with something plausible.

### 1. Which org actually does this work?

Large employers are federations, not monoliths. There is almost always a named
research organization, a specific division, or a product line where the
candidate's work belongs — and it frequently recruits on a separate track from
the booth.

Look for: a named research center or lab, the division that owns the relevant
product, the site where that work physically happens.

Naming it is the single strongest signal available in a ten-second exchange.
It's the difference between "I'm interested in your company" and "I know your
materials work sits at the site in [city] rather than in the business units."

### 2. What is the booth actually staffed for?

Check the fair's employer listing carefully. Booths are often staffed for one
named early-career program — a rotational track, a specific internship pipeline
— that may not fit the candidate at all.

**When there's a mismatch, name it in the Open.** "I see [program] posted — is
there a separate track for [what I am]?" reads as competence, not as a
complaint. Pretending the mismatch doesn't exist wastes the conversation.

### 3. What is this field currently arguing about?

Find one unresolved question practitioners actually debate — a methodology
shift, a regulatory change, a build-versus-buy tension, a technology transition
that's partly done. Ask about it.

This is what makes a technical person at the table lean in, because it's the
first question that night that wasn't answerable from the careers page.

### 4. What's the honest fit?

Rate it and say it plainly, including when it's weak. Three outcomes:

- **Strong** — the work matches. Rank it early, before lines build.
- **Adjacent** — different domain, same problem shape. Write an explicit bridge
  sentence: name the difference, then name what transfers.
- **Weak** — nothing matching. Say so. Offer it as a rehearsal rep before the
  booths that count, or say skip it.

## Sources, roughly in order of usefulness

1. **The fair's own employer listing.** Job titles, class years, work
   authorization notes, which session they attend. This is where you find that
   the booth is staffed for one specific program.
2. **The company's research or engineering blog.** What they're proud of, and
   which teams have names.
3. **Recent job postings** for roles near the candidate's target. Team names,
   site locations, and the vocabulary the company uses internally.
4. **Recent press and earnings commentary** for what's growing and what's cut.
5. **The candidate's own network.** If they have a connection inside, that
   outranks everything above.

## Guardrails

**Never invent a lab name, a site location, a product line, or a person.** A
candidate who confidently names a research center that doesn't exist has done
themselves real damage in front of someone who works there.

**Mark uncertainty explicitly.** If you're not sure the site is in the city you
think, write the hook as a question: "Does the materials work still sit at the
research center, or has that moved into the business units?" A question costs
nothing if wrong and works just as well if right.

**Don't ask about anything restricted.** For defense and classified work, frame
interest around the physics or engineering problem, never the program.

**Recency matters.** Reorganizations, acquisitions and site closures make
research stale fast. Flag anything that depends on a fact older than a year or
so with a "verify before you go" note.

## Booth order

Order the ranking by fit, not by interest, and put it in the Start tab.

Two real constraints drive this. Lines grow through the evening, so the best
fits should be hit early. And candidate energy degrades — the pitch at 8pm is
measurably worse than the one at 5pm.

If there's a genuine no-fit booth in the list, it has one legitimate use: a
free rehearsal rep. One conversation where nothing is at stake, to hear the
pitch out loud and find the word that makes eyes glaze. Put it first, and say
that's what it's for.
